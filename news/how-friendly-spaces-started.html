How Friendly Spaces started

July 2026 · Jazmine, founder

Two autumns ago I had a frustrating day out with my daughter. She was eighteen months old and riding in our hiking backpack. Nothing that happened that day was dramatic. It was the sum of small things: an entrance for families that nobody mentioned, a queue in which a baby on your back is your problem, staff who looked straight through us. By late morning I was stuck standing somewhere windy with a crying toddler, feeling that public life in this country is not made with families in mind.

I talked about it for days afterwards, with my husband and with friends who have kids. Everyone had a story like it. What nobody had was a way to say what family-friendly actually means, concretely, in a way that a place could act on. It is easy to say somewhere is not family-friendly, it requires more effort to say what would make it so. Somewhere in those conversations I realised I did not want to complain about places. I wanted the standard to change. I spoke with a friend who’s job is safety, the kind with checklists and inspections, and at some point he said: so write the checklist. Audit them. And just like that Friendly Spaces was born.

A few weeks earlier, in Japan

I should say that I had been primed. A few weeks earlier my family and I had been in Japan, our first trip there since our daughter was born. We planned the way toddler parents plan: a park near the hotel if possible, apprehension of long queues, trying to balance museum visits with time somewhere fun for her. None of it was needed. There was a family fast-track at passport control. The lifts had signs giving priority to buggies. Department stores and station buildings had family rooms, with a nursing room, changing tables that fathers could access as well as mothers, and often a padded corner where a toddler could be let loose. Every one of those rooms had a hot-water dispenser for formula (dispensing at the perfect temperature). Somewhere around the fourth day we stopped checking whether a place would work for us and started worrying about how we would readjust back home.

In Kofu, a small city on our tour, we found an indoor playground, Ventforet Oshiroland. There was a little play area right at the entrance so our daughter could already begin playing while we paid and the assistant explained how it all worked. Inside there was a section for babies and a bigger open space for small children, and three staff whose whole job was to play: to suggest a game, or to get a shy kid onto the slide, or to practise new developmental skills. It was subsidised by the city. It was spotless. And it had been built, the woman told us, on the idea that parents should come to engage with their children and meet each other.

That line stayed with me longer than anything else from the trip, because the first year of motherhood had been at times very lonely. I was lucky: a friend gave birth to her daughter around the same time, and we kept each other going with five-minute WhatsApp voice notes throughout the day. Plenty of mothers do not have that friend, and if you have no family nearby, the days can be very long. A place you can go with a baby, where you are welcome and where there might be another adult to talk to, matters more than I would have believed before I had one.

A checklist instead of a café

When we got home I wanted to build that place. A kids' café: coffee for the parents, a padded floor for the children, something for even the youngest of babies. I planned it in some detail. Then came that day out with the hiking backpack, and the conversations after it, and suddenly I had a different thought. One café would help the families in one city. A standard could elevate cafés all over the country. What if, instead of opening one place, I helped the places that already existed become visible and somewhere you would actually want to take a child, and then create a network of parents?

Switzerland has a standard for almost everything. There are labels for organic eggs, for energy-efficient fridges, for hiking trails, for hotels, for wine. There was no label that told a family whether a place would work for them. So I created one. I started the business plan in February 2025 and spent the spring refining the concept. With some focus, the Friendly Spaces audit became a point-by-point checklist of what makes a place work for a family. Some of it is obvious once you say it out loud: a highchair, a changing table available to both mothers and fathers, healthy kids food options, etc. Some of it only shows when you stand in the room with a buggy and look around. The list exists so that every location is held to the same standard.

Finding the first partner locations

Then I had to find places who wanted to join. That summer I wrote to a handful of cafés in Bern and explained the idea and why I could not stop thinking about it. By then I was pregnant with our second, which gave the whole thing a deadline I had not planned for. I expected more convincing would be needed. What I got was the owner of the Orangerie Elfenau saying, let’s do this. They signed on in August 2025. They believed families should be a bigger priority in this country, and they treated the audit as seriously as I did and were the first pilot for the Friendly Spaces Playbox. Those first partners made the launch possible, and they gave me confidence at an early point in the project.

That was just over a year ago. The pilot ended this summer, eight venues in Bern and Basel, and the launch of the app and new website. The standard exists now. The next part is filling up the map with more locations who believe in the project.

Jazmine founded Friendly Spaces in 2025. If you run a place that could be Friendly, write to her.
