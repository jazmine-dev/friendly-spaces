How Friendly Spaces started

September 2026 · Jazmine, founder

Two autumns ago I took a gondola up a mountain with my daughter on my back. She was eighteen months old and riding in one of those hiking carriers that turn a parent into a slow but determined camel. At the valley station there was a separate entrance for buggies and families. Nobody mentioned it. I found out later, the way you find out most things about a place, which is after you have already done it the hard way.

The cabin was full, and the crowd waiting for it had that day-trip energy where nobody wants to be the one left behind. I got elbowed, politely, the Swiss way, by people who could see there was a baby on my back and had decided that this was my problem. At the middle station the gondola got smaller and the crowd got faster. People jogged past us to make sure they made it on. The man loading the cabin counted heads, asked the party of three ahead of us whether they were together, and squeezed them in. He did not look at me or my baby. Then the wind came up, my daughter started to cry, and I stood on a concrete platform in the middle of a mountain being looked at sideways.

At the top there was a play corner. It was down by the toilets.

I called my husband from the summit, angry, and told him I was going to contact the company and tell them they could do better. He asked what exactly I would tell them. Which was a fair question. It is easy to say a place is not family-friendly and hard to say what that means in a way that someone could act on. His job is safety, the kind with checklists and inspections, and he said: so give them a checklist. Audit them. That is, more or less, how Friendly Spaces started.

A few weeks earlier, in Japan

I should say that I had been primed. A few weeks earlier we had been in Japan, our first trip there since our daughter was born. We planned the way toddler parents plan: a park near the hotel if possible, and a dread of every long queue. None of it was needed. There was a family fast-track at passport control. The lifts had signs giving priority to buggies. Department stores and station buildings had family rooms, with a nursing room, changing tables that fathers could use as well as mothers, and often a padded corner where a toddler could be let loose. Every one of those rooms had a hot-water dispenser for formula. Somewhere around the fourth day we stopped checking whether a place would work for us and started worrying about how we would readjust to home.

In Kofu, a small city on our route, we found an indoor playground, Ventforet Oshiroland. There was a little play area right at the entrance so our daughter could be occupied while we paid and the woman at the desk explained how it worked. Inside there was a section for babies and a bigger open space for small children, and three staff whose whole job was to play: to suggest a game, or to get a shy kid onto the slide, or to practise new skills. It was subsidised by the city. It was spotless. And it had been built, the woman told us, on the idea that parents should come to engage with their children and meet each other.

That line stayed with me longer than anything else from the trip, because the first year of motherhood had been at times very lonely. The hardest part, for me, was the afternoons. I was lucky: a friend was pregnant at the same time, and we kept each other going with five-minute WhatsApp voice notes. Plenty of mothers do not have that friend, and if you have no family nearby, the days can be very long. A place you can go with a baby, where you are welcome and where there might be another adult to talk to, matters more than I would have believed before I had one.

A checklist instead of a café

When we got home I wanted to build that place. A kids' café: coffee for the parents, a padded floor for the children. I planned it in some detail. Then came the gondola, and the phone call, and a different thought. One café would help the families who could get to it. A standard could lift cafés all over the country. What if, instead of opening one more place, I helped the places that already existed become somewhere you would actually want to take a child, and then told parents which ones they were?

Switzerland has a standard for almost everything. There are labels for organic eggs, for energy-efficient fridges, for hiking trails, for hotels, for wine. There was no label that told a family whether a place would work for them. So I wrote one. I started the business plan in February 2025 and spent the spring building it out. Over those months the Friendly Spaces audit became a point-by-point checklist of what makes a place work for a family. Some of it is obvious once you say it out loud: a high chair, a changing table in the men's room as well as the women's. Some of it took a while to see. Where a play corner is placed matters more than what is in it; next to the toilets, out of sight of the tables, is where you put something you would rather not have. The list exists so that every location is held to the same standard.

Someone had to be first

Then I had to ask someone to be first. That summer I wrote to a handful of cafés in Bern and explained the idea and why I could not stop thinking about it. By then I was pregnant with our second, which gave the whole thing a deadline I had not planned for. I expected more convincing would be needed. What I got was the owner of the Orangerie Elfenau saying: let’s do this. They signed on in August 2025. They believed families should be a bigger priority in this country, and they treated the audit as seriously as I did. Those first partners made the launch possible, and they gave me confidence at an early point in the project.

That was just over a year ago. The pilot finished in the spring, eight venues in Bern and Basel, and the app and the new website are live. I still have not written to the gondola company. I think I will, now that I have something to send them.

Jazmine founded Friendly Spaces in Bern. If you run a place that could be Friendly, write to her.
